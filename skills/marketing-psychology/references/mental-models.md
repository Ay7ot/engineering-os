# Mental model catalogue

Each entry gives the model, the psychology in one line, and a marketing
application. Pull the one or two that fit the diagnosis; do not treat this as a
checklist to apply wholesale.

## Thinking models
- First principles: reduce the problem to basic truths. Ask "why" repeatedly before copying a competitor tactic.
- Jobs to be done: people hire a product to make progress. A drill buyer wants a hole, so frame around the job.
- Circle of competence: stay where you have real advantage. Do not chase every channel.
- Inversion: ask what would guarantee failure, then remove those things.
- Occam's razor: check the obvious first. A broken form explains a conversion drop better than an attribution mystery.
- Pareto principle: about 80 percent of results come from 20 percent of effort. Find and protect the vital few.
- Local vs global optima: optimising subject lines is pointless if email is the wrong channel. Zoom out first.
- Theory of constraints: find the one bottleneck. If traffic is low, more conversion work will not help.
- Opportunity cost: every choice spends time you could spend elsewhere. Compare against the alternative.
- Diminishing returns: the tenth blog post is not the first. Know when to diversify.
- Second-order thinking: a flash sale raises revenue then trains buyers to wait for discounts.
- Map is not the territory: dashboards and personas are models, not the customer. Stay in contact with real users.
- Probabilistic thinking: plan for the campaign that underperforms, not only the one that works.
- Barbell strategy: mostly proven channels, a small slice on high-upside bets, little in between.

## How buyers think
- Fundamental attribution error: when customers do not convert, suspect the process before blaming them.
- Mere exposure: familiarity breeds preference. Consistent brand presence compounds.
- Availability heuristic: vivid, recent examples feel more likely. Case studies make success imaginable.
- Confirmation bias: people seek evidence for what they already believe. Align with existing belief rather than fighting it.
- Lindy effect: the longer an idea has lasted, the longer it will last. Fundamentals outlive tactics.
- Mimetic desire: people want what others want. Waitlists and exclusivity trigger it.
- Sunk cost fallacy: know when to kill an underperforming campaign rather than defend past spend.
- Endowment effect: people value what they already hold. Trials, samples and freemium create ownership.
- IKEA effect: people value what they helped build. Let users configure or customise.
- Zero-price effect: free is psychologically different from cheap. The step from $1 to $0 beats $2 to $1.
- Hyperbolic discounting: immediate rewards dominate. Lead with "start saving time today" over six-month ROI.
- Status-quo bias: change feels risky. Make switching safe and easy, for example one-click import.
- Default effect: pre-selected options win. Set the default you would defend.
- Paradox of choice: too many options paralyse. Three tiers beat seven.
- Goal-gradient effect: effort rises near the goal. Show progress and "almost there".
- Peak-end rule: experiences are judged by the peak and the end. Design a memorable peak and a strong finish.
- Zeigarnik effect: unfinished tasks occupy the mind. "You are 80 percent done" pulls people back.
- Pratfall effect: a small admitted weakness makes a competent brand more likeable.
- Curse of knowledge: once you know it, you cannot imagine not knowing. Test copy on outsiders.
- Mental accounting: money is treated by source and purpose. "$3 a day" lands differently from "$90 a month".
- Regret aversion: guarantees and trials reduce the fear of a bad decision.
- Bandwagon effect: popularity signals quality. Counts, reviews and logos create confidence.

## Persuasion
- Reciprocity: give first. Free tools, content and generous tiers create obligation.
- Commitment and consistency: small commitments precede large ones. Email signup, then trial, then paid.
- Authority: credentials and expertise create trust. Endorsements, certifications, "featured in".
- Liking and similarity: people say yes to those they like. Founder stories and community language signal kinship.
- Unity: shared identity drives influence. Insider language and shared values position you inside the tribe.
- Scarcity and urgency: limited availability raises perceived value. Use only when genuine.
- Foot in the door: start small and escalate. Trial, paid, annual, enterprise.
- Door in the face: open with a large ask and retreat. Enterprise pricing first makes the starter plan feel like a deal.
- Loss aversion: frame what is lost by not acting, when that framing is honest.
- Anchoring: the first number dominates. Show the higher reference price first.
- Decoy effect: a clearly worse third option makes the intended tier look obvious.
- Framing: "90 percent success" and "10 percent failure" are identical facts with different effects. Frame positively.
- Contrast effect: show the "before" so the "after" is vivid.

## Pricing psychology
- Charm pricing: prices ending in 9 read as markedly lower. Use for value-focused products.
- Rounded-price fluency: round numbers feel premium and process easily. Use round prices for premium products.
- Rule of 100: under $100 prefer percentage discounts, over $100 prefer absolute ones. "$16 off" loses to "20 percent off" on an $80 product, and wins on a $500 product.
- Price relativity: a middle tier looks reasonable between cheap and expensive. Three tiers, middle is the target.
- Mental accounting for price: "$1 a day" feels smaller than "$30 a month" for the same amount.

## Design and delivery
- Hick's law: decision time grows with the number of choices. One clear call to action beats three.
- AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action. Capture attention before building desire.
- Rule of seven: prospects need roughly seven touchpoints. One ad rarely converts.
- Nudge theory: defaults, ordering and reduced friction guide choice without restricting it.
- BJ Fogg model: behaviour needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment.
- EAST: make the behaviour easy, attractive, social and timely.
- COM-B: capability, opportunity and motivation must all be present.
- Activation energy: high starting friction blocks an otherwise easy task. Pre-fill, template, show a quick win.
- North star metric: one metric that captures delivered value, aligned across teams.
- Cobra effect: incentives can backfire. A referral bonus may attract gaming.

## Growth and scaling
- Feedback loops: output becomes input. More users, more content, better search, more users.
- Compounding: consistent small gains accumulate. Start early, benefits grow.
- Network effects: the product gets better as more people use it. Design for shared workspaces, integrations and marketplaces.
- Flywheel: sustained effort creates self-maintaining momentum. Hard to start, easy to keep.
- Switching costs: integrations, accumulated data and team adoption raise retention. Raise them ethically.
- Exploration vs exploitation: keep the working channel, fund experiments with a defined slice.
- Critical mass: growth becomes self-sustaining past a threshold. Reach depth in one segment before breadth.
- Survivorship bias: study failures too. The viral hit you copy had many unseen failures beside it.

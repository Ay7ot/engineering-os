# Working Backwards

## When to use
- Defining a new product, feature, or big bet, before any build.
- Writing a PR/FAQ or a product proposal.
- A solution exists and the team is hunting for a problem to justify it.
- Planning from a future state, or fixing a value proposition.

For turning an agreed change into a delivery spec, use `to-prd`. For mapping
opportunities against one outcome, use `opportunity-solution-tree`.

## Method
1. **Customer first.** Ask who the customer is and what problem they have, in
   the customer's words. Write the problem paragraph before any solution.
2. **Press release.** Draft the announcement for the finished product: problem
   paragraph, solution paragraph, one customer quote, one leadership quote. It
   must read as something a customer genuinely needs.
3. **FAQ.** Answer the internal questions: feasibility, cost, strategy fit,
   dependencies, and the elephant in the room. Be honest about what is hard.
4. **Three tests.** Is it a big idea, is this the right company to do it, is
   there a legitimate plan to succeed? All three must pass. The FAQ is the
   evidence for the third.
5. **Backcast.** Stand in the future state, describe the world where this
   worked, and list backwards what had to happen to get there.
6. **Theoretical ideal.** Ask what the best possible outcome is (lowest cost,
   highest speed, best experience), not the next incremental improvement, and
   work back from that.

## Rules
- Start from the problem. Never retrofit a problem to a solution already chosen.
- Trust that serving the customer well produces the business result; do not
  optimise the press release for the metric.
- Include go-to-market, organisational alignment, and stakeholder buy-in in the
  proposal, not only the product.
- Write the draft before the review meeting, so the meeting argues about the
  document rather than the idea in the abstract.

## Questions to ask the user
- Who is the customer, and what is their problem, in their words?
- What would the press release headline be?
- What would a customer quote say about how this changed their life?
- Is this a big idea, and are we the right company to build it?
- What is the elephant in the room the FAQ must answer?
- What does the theoretical ideal look like, not just an improvement?

## Common mistakes
- Starting with the solution and back-filling a problem to fit.
- A press release with vague customer benefit.
- Skipping the FAQ, so feasibility and strategy fit stay unexamined.
- Incremental thinking instead of working back from the ideal.
- Treating it as product-only, ignoring go-to-market and alignment.

## Checklist
- [ ] Problem paragraph written before the solution
- [ ] Press release has problem, solution, customer quote, leadership quote
- [ ] FAQ answers feasibility, cost, strategy fit, and the hardest question

- [ ] Go-to-market, alignment, and stakeholders covered

# Cold Email Writing

## When to use
- Writing cold outreach, prospecting or sales development emails.
- Writing subject lines, opening lines or a multi-touch follow-up sequence.
- Fixing outreach where nobody is replying.

## Before writing
- Read the project marketing context document (for example `docs/marketing-context.md`) first.
- Gather who you are writing to (role, company, why them specifically); the outcome you want (meeting, reply, intro, demo); the specific problem you solve for people like them; your proof (a result, case study or credibility signal); and any research signal (funding, hiring, posts, company news, stack changes).
- Work with what you have. A strong signal plus a clear value proposition is enough; do not block on missing inputs, just note what would make the email stronger.

## Principles
- Write like a peer, not a vendor. Use contractions, read it aloud, and rewrite anything that sounds like marketing copy.
- Every sentence must earn its place. Cold email is ruthlessly short; the best ones feel like they could have been shorter.
- Personalisation must connect to the problem. If the email still makes sense without the personalised opening, the personalisation is not working.
- Lead with their world, not yours. "You" and "your" should dominate over "I" and "we"; do not open with who you are or what your company does.
- One ask, low friction. Interest-based CTAs ("Worth exploring?", "Would this be useful?") beat meeting requests. One CTA per email, answerable in a single line.

## Voice and tone
- Target voice: a smart colleague who noticed something relevant. Conversational not sloppy, confident not pushy.
- Calibrate: C-suite ultra-brief and peer-level; mid-level more specific value and detail; technical precise and no fluff.
- It should not read like a template with fields swapped in, a pitch deck in paragraph form, or a LinkedIn-style DM to a stranger. Avoid "I hope this email finds you well", "I came across your profile", "leverage", "synergy" and "best-in-class".

## Structure
No single structure is right; pick one that fits or write freeform. Common shapes:
- Observation to Problem to Proof to Ask: "You noticed X, which usually means Y challenge. We helped Z with that. Interested?"
- Question to Value to Ask: "Struggling with X? We do Y. Company Z saw [result]. Worth a look?"
- Trigger to Insight to Ask: "Congrats on X, which usually creates Y challenge. We have helped similar companies. Curious?"
- Story to Bridge to Ask: "[Similar company] had [problem] and solved it this way. Relevant to you?"

## Subject lines
- 2-4 words, lowercase, no punctuation tricks, no product pitch, no urgency, no emoji, no prospect first name.
- They should look like an internal email from a colleague ("reply rates", "hiring ops", "Q2 forecast"). The only job is to get the email opened.

## Follow-ups
- 3-5 emails total with increasing gaps, each adding something new: a different angle, fresh proof, or a useful resource. "Just checking in" gives no reason to reply.
- Each email should stand alone, since they may not have read the previous ones. The breakup email is the last touch; honour it.

## Quality check
- Read it aloud: does it sound human? Would you reply to it? Does every sentence serve the reader rather than the sender? Is the personalisation connected to the problem? Is there one clear, low-friction ask?
- Avoid: "I hope this email finds you well" or "My name is X and I work at Y" openers; jargon such as "synergy", "leverage", "circle back" and "best-in-class"; feature dumps (one proof point beats ten features); HTML, images or multiple links; fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" subjects; identical templates with only the first name swapped; asking for a 30-minute call on first touch; "just checking in" follow-ups.

## Checklist
- [ ] Written to a named person with a real, relevant signal
- [ ] "You" and "your" dominate over "I" and "we"; no company introduction
- [ ] Personalisation is tied to the problem
- [ ] One low-friction ask, answerable in one line
- [ ] Subject is 2-4 words, lowercase, with no pitch
- [ ] Follow-ups each add new value; breakup email honoured

# Fundraising

## When to use
- Deciding whether to raise venture capital at all.
- Preparing to meet investors or start a round.
- Building resilience for the volume of rejections.
- Choosing the strongest proof point to lead with.

## Start by questioning the raise
Before optimising a pitch, check that venture capital fits the business.
The venture path sets a growth treadmill: expectations compound with each round.
Ask whether the market size, growth rate and timeline actually support it, and
what bootstrapping or revenue funding would look like as an alternative. Raising
by default is the most common early mistake.

## Lead with your strongest point on slide one
Investors form an impression in the first minute, and the first slide is the
most important one. Put your single strongest asset there:
- Strong traction: lead with the metric and its growth rate.
- A unique insight: lead with the insight.
- An exceptional team: lead with founder-market fit.
Do not bury the lede on slide five. A problem, then solution, then traction
opening is weaker than opening with the evidence itself when the evidence is
strong. For how the rest of the deck is ordered see `pitch-deck` and
`yc-pitch-deck`.

## Prepare for the dance of a hundred nos
Fundraising is a numbers game. Expect most investors to pass, often 50 to 100
conversations before a yes. Book volume so no single no feels final. Early nos
say little about the company; look for patterns in the reasons, and change only
what repeats. Keep a simple tracker of investor, stage, next step and reason so
the pattern is visible.

## Questions to ask the founder
- "What is your strongest proof point right now: traction, team, insight or market?"
- "Why venture capital specifically? Have you considered alternatives?"
- "What is on your first slide, and is it your strongest point?"
- "How many investors have you spoken to, and what patterns are in their feedback?"
- "What is the target raise, and how did you arrive at that number?"

## Common mistakes
- Burying the strongest evidence deep in the deck.
- Raising by default without weighing bootstrapping or other funding.
- Underestimating rejection, so early passes feel like verdicts.
- Opening with problem and solution when strong traction would be more compelling.
- Taking a wide range to market instead of a specific amount tied to milestones.

## Checklist
- [ ] Venture path compared against alternatives before pitching
- [ ] Single strongest proof point chosen and on slide one
- [ ] Round size tied to milestones, not a range
- [ ] Rejection volume planned for, not treated as signal
- [ ] Feedback patterns tracked across conversations

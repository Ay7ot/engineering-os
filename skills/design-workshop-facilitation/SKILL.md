# Design Workshop Facilitation

A workshop is a milestone that produces a decision or an artefact, not a
discussion. Structure it backwards from the outcome you need to leave with.

## When to use
- Framing a complex problem and aligning on a problem statement.
- Generating and narrowing solution ideas.
- Mapping a customer journey or an end-to-end experience.
- Aligning stakeholders on vision, goals or principles.
- Running a multi-day sprint from problem to tested prototype.

For feedback on an existing design, use `design-critique`. For turning the
outcome into a plan, use `project-planning`.

## Step 1: write the overview
Answer four questions before booking anything:
- Goal: two or three sentences on why this session exists.
- People: keep the group at 5 to 8 so everyone contributes. Include people who
  know the problem deeply, and for journey maps include customer-facing roles.
- Outcomes: list the concrete deliverables, for example "a concept sketch from
  each participant", "a prioritised problem list", "a journey map with pain
  points", "a draft principles document".
- When and where: 1 to 2 hours for simple sessions, 4 to 5 hours for deep
  dives, multi-day for sprints. In person: wall space and whiteboards. Remote: a
  shared virtual whiteboard. Hybrid: make the virtual board the canvas for
  everyone.

## Step 2: build the agenda
Chunk activities into blocks, then add a 20 to 30 percent buffer:

1. Introduction, 5 to 10 min: welcome, agenda, warm-up.
2. Context, 10 to 15 min: problem statement, research, constraints.
3. Main activities, varies by type (see `references/activities.md`).
4. Synthesis, 10 to 15 min: group by theme, find patterns, prioritise.
5. Next steps, 5 to 10 min: assign actions, schedule follow-ups, thanks.

Delegate sections: have the product owner present the problem and a researcher
share insights. Prepare the board with a frame and instructions per activity.
Problem statement, journey map and remote board templates are in
`references/templates.md`.

## Step 3: run it
- Announce the time limit for each activity. Watch the clock subtly; no
  countdown timers.
- Start with a warm-up, use think, pair, share for quiet participants, go
  around the room, and time-box shares to cap dominant voices.
- Set ground rules up front (devices, speaking time). Park tangents on a
  parking lot list and clear them at the end or after.
- Break every 60 to 90 minutes. Summarise after each section.
- Assign a note taker. Photograph boards. Capture all ideas, not just the good
  ones, and record decisions and owners as they happen.

## Common problems
| Problem | Response |
|---|---|
| One person dominates | silent brainstorming first, then time-boxed shares; go around in order |
| Group drifts off topic | park it visibly, redirect, schedule a follow-up if it matters |
| Quiet participants | warm-up exercise, think, pair, share, invite specific people |
| Activities overrun | the 20 to 30 percent buffer, cut the lowest-value activity |
| Ideas feel scattered | synthesis time, cluster by theme, dot vote to narrow |
| No decision reached | state the decision rule before the activity that needs it |

## After
Send a recap within 24 hours: what was done, key insights, decisions, actions
with owners, and the artefacts. Schedule the follow-up before people leave.

## Checklist
- [ ] Goal, participants and tangible outcomes written down
- [ ] Agenda built with timings plus a 20 to 30 percent buffer
- [ ] Board or slides prepared, one frame per activity
- [ ] Prep materials sent in advance
- [ ] Note taker assigned and technology tested (remote)
- [ ] Ground rules set and parking lot used
- [ ] Ideas documented and synthesised into a shortlist
- [ ] Actions assigned with owners and recap sent within 24 hours

# Content ideation sources

Use these in order when the user has the data; skip to the next when they do not.

## 1. Keyword exports

Given a CSV export (ranking tool, search console), group and tag rather than reading rows:

- Cluster related queries into topics before judging any single keyword.
- Tag each cluster by buyer stage (awareness, consideration, decision, implementation) and intent (informational, commercial, transactional).
- Flag quick wins: low competition, decent volume, high relevance to the product.
- Flag content gaps: queries competitors rank for that the site does not cover.

Return a prioritised table with columns: keyword, volume, difficulty, buyer stage, content type, priority.

## 2. Sales and customer call transcripts

Extract, then quote:

- Questions asked, which become FAQ content or posts.
- Pain points, written in the customer's own words.
- Objections, which become content that answers them before the call.
- Language patterns, which become exact phrasing to reuse (voice of customer).
- Competitor mentions, which show what they compared you against.

Return content ideas with the supporting quote attached to each.

## 3. Survey responses

Mine for:

- Open-ended answers, for topics and phrasing.
- Common themes: treat 30 percent or more of mentions as high priority.
- Resource requests: what respondents wish existed.
- Format preferences: written, video or audio.

## 4. Forum research

Search public communities for problems rather than products:

- Reddit: `site:reddit.com [topic]`, then read top posts and the comments, where the real questions sit.
- Q&A sites: most-followed questions and highly upvoted answers.
- Other communities: founder forums, news aggregators, launch sites, industry chat servers.

Extract FAQs, misconceptions, live debates, problems being solved, and the terminology the community uses.

## 5. Competitor content

- Find the blog with a site-restricted search, then look at what repeatedly performs (comments, shares, syndication).
- Note topics covered repeatedly, gaps they skip, the case studies they publish, and their structure (pillars, categories, formats).
- Identify where you can cover a topic better, which angles they miss, and which of their pages are outdated enough to beat.

## 6. Customer-facing teams

Ask support, success and sales for:

- Common objections and repeated questions.
- Support ticket patterns.
- Success stories with numbers.
- Feature requests, and the underlying problem behind each.

## Turning a source into a plan

- De-duplicate: the same idea often arrives from two sources; keep the version with the strongest evidence.
- Attach evidence to every idea so prioritisation is not opinion.
- Prefer topics with both demand (search or conversation volume) and a unique angle you can defend.
